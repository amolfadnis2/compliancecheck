Both in-house and outsourced payroll can be run compliantly, but they shift where the compliance workload and the compliance risk sit - and that trade-off, not just cost, is what should drive the decision.

## Key facts at a glance

- **Employer liability for PF, ESI and TDS never transfers** to a payroll vendor - only the processing work does.
- **EPF is mandatory once a business crosses 20 employees**, with a Rs 15,000/month wage ceiling for coverage.
- **ESI is mandatory once a business crosses 10 employees** (1+ in hazardous units), with a Rs 21,000/month wage ceiling.
- Most **payroll vendors charge per employee per month**, so cost scales roughly linearly with headcount.
- **Multi-state operations multiply complexity** - professional tax rules, minimum wages and shop and establishment rules vary by state.
- A **dedicated in-house payroll hire** typically only becomes cost-competitive with outsourcing once headcount is large enough to keep that role fully occupied.
- The **Labour Codes transition** (effective 21 November 2025, state rules still rolling out) is changing wage definitions in ways that make payroll logic harder to keep current manually.

## What "In-House Payroll" Actually Involves

Running payroll in-house means someone on your team (often the founder, an HR generalist, or an accountant) calculates wages, deductions and statutory contributions every cycle, then files PF, ESI, TDS and professional tax returns on time. At low headcounts this can work fine with a spreadsheet or basic software. As headcount grows, the number of moving parts - overtime rules, leave encashment, gratuity accruals, multi-state professional tax slabs - grows faster than most founders expect.

The core risk with in-house payroll is not incompetence, it is bandwidth. Statutory deadlines do not move for a busy month, and a missed PF or ESI filing deadline can trigger interest and penalties regardless of intent.

## What "Outsourced Payroll" Actually Involves

Outsourcing means handing wage calculation, payslip generation, and statutory filings to a vendor or payroll software platform, sometimes with a dedicated account manager. The employer still supplies the source data (attendance, salary structure, joiners and leavers) and still owns the legal obligation to remit and file correctly. A good vendor reduces the chance of a missed deadline or a miscalculated contribution because that is their core, repeated function rather than a side task.

The trade-off is less direct control and a recurring cost that scales with headcount, plus dependency on a third party's data security and responsiveness.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| Factor | In-house payroll | Outsourced payroll |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal liability for PF/ESI/TDS | Stays with employer | Stays with employer |
| Day-to-day processing effort | Internal team's time | Vendor's time |
| Cost structure | Fixed salary + software licence | Usually per-employee-per-month fee |
| Multi-state complexity handling | Depends on internal expertise | Vendor typically tracks state rules |
| Speed to adapt to Labour Code changes | Depends on internal awareness | Vendor usually updates faster |
| Data control | Fully internal | Shared with a third party |
| Best fit | Very small, single-state teams | Growing, multi-state, or resource-constrained teams |

## When In-House Still Makes Sense

For a very small, single-state team with straightforward salary structures, in-house payroll on basic software can be entirely adequate, especially if a founder or finance lead already has payroll experience. The calculations are simple enough that error risk is low, and the cost of a vendor may not be justified yet.

## When Outsourcing Starts to Pay Off

As headcount crosses the EPF (20 employees) or ESI (10 employees) thresholds, or as the business expands into a second state, the compliance surface area grows faster than a generalist can track reliably. This is usually the point where outsourcing - or at least dedicated payroll software with compliance updates built in - starts to reduce risk more than it costs.

## The One Thing Neither Option Removes

Whichever model you choose, the business remains the legal employer of record for statutory purposes. A vendor's mistake is still your penalty exposure. That makes periodic review of what is actually being filed, not just trust in the arrangement, a necessary habit either way.
