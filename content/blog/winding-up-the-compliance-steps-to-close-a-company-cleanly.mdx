Closing an Indian company properly is not as simple as stopping operations - it requires clearing statutory dues, cancelling registrations, and filing the correct ROC form, and skipping these steps leaves directors exposed to penalties and personal liability long after the business has stopped trading.

## Key facts at a glance

- The fast-track route for a defunct company with no assets or liabilities is **Form STK-2** (strike-off) filed with the **Registrar of Companies**.
- Companies with assets to distribute or creditors to settle generally need formal **liquidation**, not simplified strike-off.
- All **overdue ROC filings and statutory dues** must generally be cleared before a strike-off application is accepted.
- **GST registration cancellation** is a separate filing on the GST portal, requiring a final GST return.
- **PF and ESI accounts** must be settled, with employee dues cleared as part of closure.
- Directors do **not automatically escape liability** for pre-closure conduct once a company is struck off.
- A struck-off company can, in certain circumstances, be **restored** to the register if closure was improper or dues surface later.

## Choosing the right closure route

Not every company closure looks the same, and picking the wrong route wastes time and money. The two broad paths are:

### Strike-off (Form STK-2) - for dormant, asset-free companies

This is the simplified, administrative route meant for companies that have effectively stopped operating, have no significant assets, and have no outstanding liabilities to creditors. It is filed with the Registrar of Companies and, if accepted, results in the company's name being removed from the register.

### Liquidation - for companies with assets or creditors

Where a company holds assets that need to be realised and distributed, or has creditors who must be paid through a structured process, formal liquidation (voluntary or through the National Company Law Tribunal, depending on circumstances) is required instead. This is a longer, more document-intensive process involving a liquidator, creditor claims, and asset distribution.

## Comparing the two routes

| Aspect | Strike-off (STK-2) | Liquidation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Best suited for | Dormant company, no assets/liabilities | Company with assets or creditors to settle |
| Filed with | Registrar of Companies | NCLT (compulsory) or via liquidator (voluntary) |
| Relative speed | Faster, but still several months | Slower, often a year or more |
| Complexity | Lower - administrative filing | Higher - formal process with liquidator |
| Outcome | Name struck off register | Company formally dissolved after distribution |

## Steps to close a company through strike-off

**1. Settle all dues and liabilities.** Clear outstanding statutory dues - tax, GST, PF, ESI, and any vendor or creditor payments. A strike-off application with pending dues is a common rejection reason.

**2. Clear overdue ROC filings.** Bring annual filings (AOC-4, MGT-7/7A) up to date. The Registrar typically expects the company to be current on its filing obligations before processing a strike-off.

**3. Close bank accounts and pass board/shareholder resolutions.** A board resolution recommending closure, followed by shareholder approval (special resolution), is generally required before filing.

**4. File Form STK-2** with the required attachments - indemnity bonds, statement of accounts, an affidavit, and the resolution - along with the prescribed fee.

**5. Notice and objection period.** The Registrar publishes a notice inviting objections from the public and other regulators before finalising the strike-off.

**6. Cancel other registrations separately.** GST registration, PF and ESI establishment codes, professional tax registration, and any sector-specific licences (FSSAI, trade licence, and so on) must be cancelled through their respective portals - these do not automatically lapse just because the company is struck off with the ROC.

## Steps specific to liquidation

Liquidation involves appointing a liquidator, settling claims in the statutory order of priority, realising and distributing assets, and obtaining final dissolution orders. Given the complexity - especially the priority order for paying employees, secured creditors, unsecured creditors, and shareholders - this route is best handled with a company secretary or insolvency professional guiding the process from the start.

## What closure does not erase

A common misconception is that strike-off or liquidation wipes the slate clean for directors. It does not. Directors remain accountable for conduct during the company's operating life, including any fraudulent trading, unpaid statutory dues discovered later, or misrepresentation in the closure filing itself. The Registrar or an affected party can, in defined circumstances, apply to **restore** a struck-off company to the register - for instance, if dues surface after closure or if the strike-off was procured improperly.

## Employees and statutory accounts on closure

Employees are entitled to their full and final settlement, including any notice pay, retrenchment compensation where applicable, and gratuity for those who qualify. PF and ESI accounts tied to the company must be properly closed or transferred, and final contribution filings submitted, before the establishment codes themselves are cancelled.

## A practical closure checklist

- Reconcile and clear all tax, GST, PF and ESI dues.
- Bring ROC annual filings current.
- Settle employee full-and-final dues.
- Cancel GST, PF/ESI, professional tax and any sector-specific licences separately.
- Choose strike-off or liquidation based on whether assets/liabilities remain.
- Retain closure documents and filing acknowledgements even after the company is struck off.
