Every **Internal Committee (ICC)** under the POSH Act must include at least one **external member** who is not an employee of the organisation, and getting this appointment wrong is one of the fastest ways to invalidate an otherwise properly run committee.

## Key facts at a glance

- The external member must come from an **NGO or association** committed to the cause of women, or be someone familiar with **sexual harassment issues**.
- The external member **cannot be a current employee** of the organisation - this is non-negotiable.
- Every ICC needs an external member regardless of company size; there is **no small-employer exemption**.
- External members are entitled to a **fee or allowance** for attending proceedings, set under the POSH Rules.
- The same individual can lawfully serve as external member for **multiple, unrelated companies**.
- An inquiry conducted without the external member present is at real risk of being **challenged as invalid**.
- ICC is mandatory wherever a workplace has **10 or more employees**.

## Why the law insists on an outsider

The external member exists specifically to bring independence and subject-matter perspective that internal committee members, who work alongside both the complainant and respondent, may struggle to provide. Their presence is meant to reduce the risk of institutional bias or pressure influencing an inquiry's outcome, which is why the law treats their inclusion as mandatory rather than optional.

## Who actually qualifies

The POSH Act sets two acceptable routes to qualification. The external member can be from a non-governmental organisation or association working on issues connected to women, or can be an individual otherwise familiar with issues relating to sexual harassment - for example, a lawyer with relevant experience, a social worker, or a subject-matter consultant. What disqualifies someone is being a current employee of the organisation; the whole point is that this member sits outside the internal reporting structure.

### Common sourcing routes

Smaller and mid-sized companies without existing NGO relationships typically engage specialised POSH compliance consultancies that maintain a roster of qualified external members available across multiple client organisations. Larger companies sometimes build a direct relationship with a local NGO. Either route is legitimate, and it is entirely normal - and legal - for one external member to sit on the ICCs of several unconnected companies simultaneously.

## What to pay them

External members are entitled to fees or allowances for the time they spend attending ICC meetings and inquiry proceedings, as prescribed under the POSH Rules. Because the exact fee structure and any caps are set through subordinate rules that can be revised, employers should confirm the current prescribed amount with a compliance professional or the Ministry of Women and Child Development rather than relying on a fixed figure that may be outdated. In practice, many organisations also cover reasonable travel expenses for the external member's attendance, on top of any prescribed sitting fee.

| Aspect | What applies |
| --- | --- |
| Who qualifies | NGO/association member on women's issues, or person familiar with sexual harassment matters |
| Who is excluded | Any current employee of the organisation |
| Payment | Fee/allowance per the POSH Rules, plus typically reasonable travel expenses |
| Can serve multiple employers | Yes, commonly does in practice |
| Consequence of absence during inquiry | Inquiry validity at risk of challenge |

## Practical steps for HR

Confirm your current external member's engagement letter clearly states their role, the fee arrangement, and their availability commitments - a vague or undocumented arrangement creates uncertainty exactly when you need clarity, mid-inquiry. If your external member is unresponsive or unavailable for extended periods, replace them promptly rather than letting inquiries proceed without them, since their absence is a structural defect, not a minor procedural gap.
