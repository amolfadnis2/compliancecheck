A fire safety audit checks whether your office, shop or restaurant has working extinguishers, clear exits, functioning alarms and a valid fire NOC, before an inspector, insurer or landlord asks you to prove it. Most gaps are cheap to fix once you know what to look for.

## Key facts at a glance

- A **Fire No Objection Certificate (NOC)** from the local fire department is typically required for commercial premises above a state-specified built-up area or occupancy load.
- Fire **extinguishers** should be visually checked monthly and professionally serviced at least once a year.
- **Exit routes and staircases** must generally stay unobstructed at all times, with only fire-rated material stored near them.
- Emergency lighting and **illuminated exit signage** are commonly required to remain functional even during a power cut.
- Restaurants face extra scrutiny for **kitchen fire risk**: LPG/gas connections, exhaust ducts and fire suppression systems above cooking lines.
- Fire safety norms sit at the intersection of the **National Building Code**, state Fire Service Acts and local municipal by-laws, so the specific checklist can differ by state and city.
- Insurance claims for fire damage are frequently reduced or rejected if the premises lacked a valid fire NOC or basic firefighting equipment at the time of loss.

## What a basic fire safety audit should cover

A useful internal audit walks the premises the way an inspector would, room by room, and checks five broad areas: statutory paperwork, firefighting equipment, exits and signage, electrical safety, and staff readiness. Treat it as a recurring exercise, not a one-time task.

### Statutory paperwork

Confirm the **Fire NOC** is current and matches the premises as actually built out (not an older layout). Keep the certificate, the approved building plan and any prior inspection reports in one accessible file, since inspectors and insurers both ask for these first.

### Firefighting equipment

Check that extinguisher type matches the risk in each area (for example, a **CO2 or dry chemical powder extinguisher** near electrical panels, and appropriate kitchen-class extinguishers near cooking lines). Every extinguisher should have a visible, current service tag.

### Exits, signage and access

Walk every escape route and confirm it is unobstructed, doors open in the direction of travel where required, and exit signage is illuminated and visible from every point on the floor. Fire doors should close and latch properly, not be wedged open.

### Electrical safety

Overloaded sockets, exposed wiring and undersized cabling are among the most common causes of commercial fires. A periodic electrical safety check by a qualified electrician is a practical complement to the fire audit itself.

### Staff readiness

Even good equipment does not help if staff do not know how to use it. Basic fire drill awareness, knowledge of assembly points, and clarity on who calls the fire brigade are simple, low-cost items that are frequently missed.

## How often should you audit

Many businesses treat fire safety as a once-a-year task tied to NOC renewal, but a lighter internal check every quarter catches problems (an expired extinguisher tag, a blocked exit, a burnt-out emergency light) well before the annual renewal cycle. Restaurants with high kitchen fire risk often benefit from more frequent checks of exhaust ducts and gas connections.

## Common gaps found in real audits

Extinguishers past their service date are the single most common finding, followed by blocked or locked emergency exits, missing or non-illuminated exit signage, and storage of flammable material (cardboard, packaging) too close to electrical panels or exit routes. None of these are expensive to fix, which is why they are usually treated as avoidable, not tolerable.
