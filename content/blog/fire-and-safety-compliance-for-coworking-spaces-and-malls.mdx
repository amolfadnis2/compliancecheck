Fire and safety compliance in coworking spaces and malls is split between two parties: the **building owner** typically holds the fire NOC for the structure, while the **operating business** (coworking operator, mall management, or individual tenant) is responsible for not violating that approval within its own space.

## Key facts at a glance

- The **Fire No Objection Certificate (NOC)** for a building is generally the responsibility of the **building owner or landlord**, not individual tenants.
- Malls and similar high-footfall premises are typically classified as **high-occupancy or assembly buildings**, triggering stricter fire safety norms than standard offices.
- Fire safety approvals are issued against a **maximum occupancy** calculated from floor area and exit capacity - exceeding it is itself a violation.
- Both the **building owner and the tenant business** can face consequences if a shared building's fire NOC lapses.
- High-occupancy buildings like malls are commonly expected to run evacuation drills **quarterly**, more often than the six-monthly norm for standard offices.
- Coworking spaces face a distinct risk: **flexible seating configurations changing faster than the underlying fire safety approval**.

## Two layers of responsibility

Fire safety compliance in shared buildings works on two layers. The **building-level layer** covers the structure itself - fire exits, sprinkler systems, fire NOC, and the overall approved occupancy - and is the landlord's or building owner's responsibility. The **tenant-level layer** covers what happens inside a leased space - not blocking exits with furniture, not overloading electrical circuits, and not exceeding the occupancy the space was designed for.

Both coworking operators and mall tenants need to understand which layer they are responsible for, because inspectors and courts do not always draw a clean line between the two when something goes wrong.

## Why malls face stricter norms

Malls, being high-footfall assembly premises, are typically classified differently from standard commercial offices under state fire safety rules. This classification usually brings:

- Higher fire load and sprinkler system requirements
- Wider or additional emergency exit requirements
- More frequent drill expectations, often quarterly rather than six-monthly
- Stricter occupancy caps relative to floor area

## Fire safety obligations compared

| Aspect | Coworking space | Mall |

| Fire NOC holder | Building owner (operator manages fit-out compliance) | Building/mall owner or management company |

## The coworking-specific occupancy risk

Coworking spaces are particularly exposed to one specific gap: the underlying fire safety approval assumes a certain occupancy based on a fixed floor layout, but coworking operators regularly reconfigure desks, cabins, and meeting rooms to maximise seating. Every time seating density increases, there is a risk of exceeding the fire-safety-approved occupancy without anyone formally checking against the original plan. Periodically reconciling actual occupancy against the approved fire safety plan is a low-effort way to catch this before an inspector does.

## What happens when a building's NOC lapses

If a shared building's fire NOC lapses - a landlord responsibility - both the landlord and the tenant businesses operating inside it can face consequences. Regulators generally do not accept "the NOC lapse was the landlord's fault" as a full defence for a tenant continuing to operate; the practical takeaway is that tenants should track their landlord's fire NOC renewal status as an active part of their own compliance calendar, not assume it is entirely someone else's problem.

## Practical steps for operators

Coworking operators and mall tenants alike benefit from periodically requesting a copy of the building's current fire NOC and drill records from the landlord or mall management, keeping their own space's fit-out plan on file, and running their own occupant-level evacuation briefings even where the building conducts its own drills.
