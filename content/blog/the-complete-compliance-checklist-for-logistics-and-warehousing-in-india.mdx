Logistics and warehousing businesses in India combine a heavily contract-driven workforce with premises that store significant value or hazardous material - a combination that puts contract labour law, fire safety, and the Labour Codes transition all near the top of the compliance list.

## Key facts at a glance

- All **4 Labour Codes** are in force nationally since **21 November 2025**, with state-level rule notification still incomplete in most states as of mid-2026.
- Engaging **20 or more contract workers** through a manpower contractor triggers principal-employer registration under contract labour law.
- **ESI** applies once an establishment reaches **10 or more employees**, with a **Rs 21,000/month** wage ceiling.
- **EPF** applies once an establishment reaches **20 or more employees**, with a **Rs 15,000/month** wage ceiling.
- A POSH **Internal Committee** is mandatory once total employees reach **10 or more**, covering warehouse, driving, and office staff.
- E-way bills are generally required for goods movement above a prescribed consignment value, with thresholds and exceptions that vary by state for intra-state transport.

## The Labour Codes transition and logistics

Logistics is a sector where the Labour Codes transition matters in a very concrete way, because it touches fixed-term employment, working hour flexibility for shift-based warehouse operations, and standing orders thresholds - all areas the new Codes intend to change. Since the Codes are in force nationally from **21 November 2025** but each state must separately notify its own rules, a logistics company operating across multiple states may find itself under the older framework in one state and a partially notified new framework in another. The safest approach is to track each operating state's notification status individually rather than apply a single national assumption across all facilities.

## Contract labour: the sector's core compliance area

Warehousing and logistics rely heavily on contract and third-party staffing for loading, sorting, and last-mile operations. Under contract labour law, once a warehouse or logistics facility engages **20 or more contract workers** through one or more contractors, the facility becomes a **principal employer** with registration obligations, and each contractor needs its own labour licence. Principal employers also carry residual liability for wage payment if a contractor defaults, which makes verifying contractor PF/ESI compliance a practical necessity, not just a formality.

| Requirement | Threshold | Key detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contract labour registration | 20+ contract workers | Principal employer registers; contractor needs a licence |
| EPF | 20+ employees | Rs 15,000 wage ceiling; 12% + 12% |
| ESI | 10+ employees | Rs 21,000 wage ceiling; 0.75% + 3.25% |
| POSH Internal Committee | 10+ employees | Covers warehouse, driving, and office staff |

## Gig and platform workforce classification

Delivery riders and last-mile drivers occupy a genuinely unsettled classification zone between employee and independent contractor. The **Code on Social Security, 2020** specifically introduces recognition of gig and platform workers as a category, with associated welfare scheme provisions, separate from the traditional employee definition that triggers PF/ESI. Logistics companies using platform-style delivery models should track how this classification develops as Codes are notified state by state, since it directly affects whether standard PF/ESI obligations apply to that workforce segment.

## Fire and warehouse safety

Warehouses storing chemicals, flammable packaging material, electronics, or high-density racking typically require a **Fire NOC**, and facilities storing hazardous materials may need additional clearances tied to storage volume and material classification. Racking safety, aisle width, and fire exit standards are commonly checked during municipal or fire department inspections, and non-compliance can lead to an inventory-value-destroying fire risk as much as a regulatory one - this is an area where compliance and business continuity genuinely overlap.

## GST, e-way bills, and inter-state movement

Logistics operations are directly exposed to **GST e-way bill** compliance, since moving goods above the prescribed consignment value threshold generally requires a valid e-way bill, with variations for intra-state movement depending on the state. Warehouses acting as transshipment or fulfilment points for multiple client businesses should have clear processes for verifying e-way bill validity on inbound and outbound shipments, since carrying goods without a valid bill exposes the vehicle and goods to detention risk, not just a penalty on paper.

## Bringing it together

For a logistics or warehousing business, the compliance stack concentrates around three things: getting contract labour registration right given the scale of outsourced staffing, tracking fire and storage safety given the physical risk profile of a warehouse, and staying current on how the Labour Codes transition affects working hours and workforce classification in each state of operation.
