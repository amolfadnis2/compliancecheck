Manufacturing SMEs in India carry one of the heaviest compliance loads of any sector - factory safety, pollution control, and a labour law framework that is mid-transition from old Acts to the new Labour Codes, all layered on the same premises.

## Key facts at a glance

- All **4 Labour Codes** are in force nationally since **21 November 2025**, but state-level rule notification is still incomplete for most states as of mid-2026.
- **EPF** remains mandatory at **20 or more employees**, with a **Rs 15,000/month** wage ceiling, unchanged under EPF Scheme 2026 (effective 29 June 2026).
- **ESI** is mandatory at **10 or more employees** (or fewer in notified hazardous units), with a **Rs 21,000/month** wage ceiling.
- Engaging **20 or more contract workers** through a contractor triggers principal-employer registration under contract labour law.
- A POSH **Internal Committee** is mandatory once total headcount reaches **10 or more employees**, including shop-floor staff.
- Pollution Control Board **Consent to Establish** and **Consent to Operate** are generally required before commencing manufacturing operations.

## The Labour Codes transition: what applies today

This is the area most manufacturers get wrong right now. The 4 Codes - Wages, Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) - are legally in force nationally, but labour is a **concurrent subject**, meaning both the central government and each state must notify their own rules before the Codes are fully operational in that state. Central rules were pre-published in draft form in late December 2025, with final Central rules expected around April 2026, but state notification has lagged.

Practically, this means a manufacturer should check their specific state's notification status rather than assume the new Codes' provisions (on things like fixed-term employment, standing orders thresholds, or retrenchment compensation) are fully governing yet - in many states, the older Acts (Factories Act, Industrial Disputes Act, Contract Labour Act) continue to apply in practice until the transition completes locally.

## Factory safety and the OSH Code

The **OSH Code** is designed to eventually consolidate 13 laws, including the Factories Act, 1948 and the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970. Until a state's OSH Code rules are notified, factories should continue meeting Factories Act obligations: factory registration and licensing, safety officer appointment above prescribed worker counts, welfare facilities (canteens, restrooms, first aid), working hour limits, and mandatory registers for accidents and overtime.

## Pollution control and environmental clearances

Manufacturing units, unlike pure office-based businesses, generally need environmental clearances before they can even begin construction or operation:

| Clearance | When required |
| --- | --- |
| Consent to Establish (CTE) | Before setting up the factory/plant |
| Consent to Operate (CTO) | Before commencing production; renewed periodically |
| Hazardous waste authorisation | If the unit generates hazardous waste |
| Fire NOC | Before occupancy, particularly for units storing flammable material |

The exact category (red/orange/green/white, based on pollution potential) determines which clearances apply and how often they are renewed - a category classification manufacturers should confirm with their state pollution control board rather than assume.

## Labour compliance and contract workforce

| Requirement | Threshold | Key detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EPF | 20+ employees | Rs 15,000 wage ceiling; 12% + 12% |
| ESI | 10+ employees (1+ in hazardous units) | Rs 21,000 wage ceiling; 0.75% + 3.25% |
| Contract labour registration | 20+ contract workers | Principal employer registers; contractor needs a licence |
| POSH Internal Committee | 10+ employees | Covers entire workforce including shop floor |

Manufacturing SMEs commonly under-comply on contract labour specifically - because contract workers are technically employed by a labour contractor, the principal employer sometimes assumes it has no direct obligation, but registration and oversight duties fall on the principal employer regardless.

## POSH on the shop floor

POSH compliance in manufacturing is frequently treated as an office-only concern, but the Act covers the entire workforce, including shop-floor and warehouse staff. With the Supreme Court pushing for district-wise POSH audits and Internal Committee registration on the government's **SHe-Box** portal, manufacturers should ensure their Internal Committee is actually functional - trained members, a documented complaint process, and awareness sessions reaching shop-floor workers, not just a policy filed away in HR.

## Bringing it together

Manufacturing sits at the busiest intersection of compliance categories: factory/OSH safety, pollution control, contract labour, and the standard PF/ESI/POSH stack, all while the underlying legal framework is mid-transition through the Labour Codes. The practical approach is to track your specific state's Labour Codes notification status separately from your day-to-day compliance calendar, since the two move on different timelines.
