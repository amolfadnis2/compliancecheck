Selling and servicing electric vehicles adds a layer of compliance on top of everything a conventional dealership already handles, centred on charging infrastructure safety and battery handling.

## Key facts at a glance

- EV dealerships carry **all standard dealership obligations** - labour law, MISP, DPDP - plus **EV-specific requirements**.
- Charging infrastructure generally falls under **electrical safety rules** enforced by the state electrical inspectorate.
- Used or damaged lithium-ion batteries are **hazardous waste** and fall under the **Battery Waste Management Rules**.
- Fixed charger installations may require **inspection or approval** before commissioning, depending on scale.
- **Lithium-ion battery fires** need different suppression approaches than fuel fires, affecting workshop fire planning.
- Staff handling **high-voltage EV systems** typically need dedicated safety training beyond standard workshop training.

## Why EV dealerships need an additional layer of compliance

A conventional dealership's compliance picture is built around fuel, mechanical systems, and standard workshop hazards. EVs introduce high-voltage electrical systems, lithium-ion battery storage, and on-site charging infrastructure, none of which map neatly onto existing dealership compliance checklists. Treating an EV dealership as "a regular dealership that happens to sell EVs" misses real risk - both regulatory and physical safety risk - that the existing framework was not built to cover.

## Charging infrastructure: what to check

Any dealership installing chargers for demonstration, customer use, or fleet charging needs to treat this as an electrical safety matter, not just a facilities upgrade. Fixed installations generally need to meet applicable wiring and electrical safety standards, and depending on scale, may require inspection or sign-off from the state electrical inspectorate before being brought into use. Guidelines from the Ministry of Power and the Bureau of Energy Efficiency are also relevant to how charging equipment should be installed and operated safely.

## Battery handling and disposal

Lithium-ion batteries - whether new stock, batteries removed during servicing, or damaged/end-of-life batteries - are classified as hazardous waste and fall under the Battery Waste Management Rules. This means a dealership cannot simply store or dispose of batteries the way it might handle other workshop waste. Batteries generally need to go through authorised collection or recycling channels, with proper storage in the interim to reduce fire risk.

## Comparing EV-specific obligations to conventional dealership obligations

| Area | Conventional dealership | EV dealership addition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fuel/energy storage | Fuel storage rules | Charging infrastructure electrical safety |
| Battery/hazardous waste | Standard workshop waste (oil, batteries) | Lithium-ion battery-specific handling and disposal |
| Staff safety training | Standard workshop safety | High-voltage system training |
| Fire safety | Fuel-fire suppression planning | Lithium-ion battery fire response planning |

## Fire safety needs rethinking, not just extending

Lithium-ion battery fires behave differently from conventional fuel fires - they can reignite, burn at high intensity, and are not always effectively managed with standard suppression equipment. A dealership's existing fire safety plan, likely built around fuel and standard electrical risk, needs a specific review for how it handles battery storage areas, charging bays, and damaged-battery holding areas, rather than assuming the existing plan already covers this.

## Staff training is a compliance gap, not just an operational nicety

Workshop staff servicing EVs are working with high-voltage systems that carry real injury risk if mishandled. Beyond general workplace safety obligations under labour law, dealerships should treat EV-specific technical safety training as part of their compliance posture, not an optional skills upgrade, particularly as inspections and insurance underwriting increasingly look for evidence of this.

## What to prioritise first

For a dealership adding EVs to its lineup, the fastest wins are getting charging infrastructure formally inspected where required, setting up a documented battery storage and disposal process with an authorised recycler, and updating the workshop's fire safety plan to specifically address battery risk, rather than treating EV compliance as an afterthought layered on the existing dealership checklist.
