FSSAI's hygiene rating scheme scores a food business on its actual kitchen and service practices, while third-party audits are how FSSAI verifies compliance without relying only on its own inspectors. Together they are becoming a routine part of running a licensed food business in India.

## Key facts at a glance

- Hygiene rating uses a **star scale of 1 to 5**, awarded after a physical on-site audit of food safety practices.
- Inspections have moved from a **fixed calendar-based system to a risk-based system**, effective under the FSSAI Licensing and Registration Amendment Regulations 2026 (effective 11 March 2026).
- **Third-party audits** by FSSAI-empanelled agencies can now be directed in place of, or alongside, government inspections.
- A hygiene rating certificate is **separate from your FSSAI licence or registration** - one does not substitute for the other.
- Ratings are subject to **periodic reassessment**, not a one-time award.
- Businesses with poor audit outcomes risk **improvement notices, licence action, or public disclosure** of their rating.

## What the hygiene rating scheme actually measures

Hygiene rating audits look at food handling, storage temperatures, staff personal hygiene, pest control, waste management, and general premises cleanliness. Auditors typically visit unannounced or with limited notice, observe live operations, and score against a standard checklist rather than just reviewing paperwork.

The resulting star rating is meant to be a consumer-facing signal, often displayed at the premises or on a delivery app listing, similar in spirit to a food safety scorecard used in several other countries.

## How third-party audits fit into risk-based inspection

Until recently, FSSAI inspections largely followed a fixed calendar, meaning a business could roughly predict when the next visit would happen. Under the 2026 amendment regulations, inspection frequency and depth are tied to a business's assessed risk - a higher-risk operation (larger scale, more complex processing, past non-compliance) faces more frequent or more thorough scrutiny.

To manage this at scale, FSSAI can direct **third-party audits** carried out by empanelled private audit agencies rather than only departmental officers. These agencies follow FSSAI-prescribed protocols and report findings back into the regulatory system, effectively expanding inspection capacity without expanding government headcount.

### Why this shift matters for smaller businesses

A predictable, low-frequency calendar previously meant many small food businesses rarely saw an inspector. Risk-based, third-party-enabled inspection removes that predictability. A business that looks compliant on paper but has poor day-to-day practices is now more likely to be caught out during an actual visit.

## What a food business should do to prepare

- Keep hygiene practices audit-ready every day, not just before an expected visit, since inspections are no longer predictable.
- Maintain visible records of pest control, water testing, and staff health checks, since auditors typically ask for these on the spot.
- Train staff on basic food safety practices so behaviour holds up under observation, not just documentation.
- Treat any improvement notice from a rating or audit seriously and close out the corrective actions within the given timeline.

## Hygiene rating versus FSSAI licence: how they differ

| Aspect | FSSAI Licence/Registration | Hygiene Rating |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Legal permission to operate | Quality/practice signal to consumers |
| Mandatory? | Yes, for all eligible food businesses | Increasingly expected, scope expanding |
| Basis | Business turnover and category | On-site audit of actual practices |
| Validity | No fixed expiry for new licences from 1 April 2026 | Periodically reassessed |
| Consequence of failure | Licence suspension or cancellation | Poor public rating, reputational impact |
