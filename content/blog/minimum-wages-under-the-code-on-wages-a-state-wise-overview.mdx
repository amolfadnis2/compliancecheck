Minimum wages in India are still set primarily at the **state level**, and they vary widely by state, zone, and skill category - even after the national **Code on Wages** came into force.

## Key facts at a glance

- The Code on Wages introduces a **national floor wage**, a baseline below which no state can set its minimum wage.
- Each **state** still fixes its own minimum wage rates, which must be at or above the floor wage.
- Rates typically differ by **skill category**: unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, and highly skilled.
- Many states also vary rates by **zone or city** (for example, metro versus non-metro classifications).
- The Code on Wages has been in force nationally since **21 November 2025**, but state-level rule notification remains uneven as of mid-2026.
- Paying below the applicable minimum wage exposes an employer to **recovery proceedings and penalties**.

## Why "minimum wage in India" is not one number

Unlike a single national minimum wage, India's system layers several variables:

- **State**: each state government notifies its own minimum wage rates.
- **Zone**: many states divide districts into zones (for example, Zone A for major cities, Zone B/C for smaller towns) with different rates.
- **Skill category**: unskilled, semi-skilled, skilled, and highly skilled workers typically have progressively higher minimum rates.
- **Scheduled employment**: certain industries or job categories may have specific notified rates that differ from the general minimum wage.

This means an employer with offices in three states, each with employees across two skill categories, could legitimately be tracking six or more different minimum wage figures.

## Illustrative structure of state-wise variation

| Variable | Example variation |
| --- | --- |
| State | Minimum wage differs across every state; some states are meaningfully higher than others |
| Zone within state | Metro/urban zones often carry higher rates than rural zones |
| Skill category | Highly skilled rates are typically well above unskilled rates within the same state |
| Revision cycle | Some states revise twice yearly linked to cost-of-living index; others revise less frequently |

Because these figures change periodically and differ so much by location, employers should always confirm the **current** notified rate for their specific state, zone, and skill category directly from the state labour department rather than relying on a fixed figure.

## The national floor wage under the Code on Wages

The **Code on Wages, 2020** introduces the concept of a **national floor wage**, set by the central government, which acts as a baseline that no state can go below when fixing its own minimum wage. States that already have a minimum wage above the floor wage are not required to reduce it - the floor wage functions as a floor, not a target.

This is intended to reduce the wide disparity between states over time and ensure a baseline standard of pay nationally, while still allowing states to set higher rates based on local cost of living and economic conditions.

## Why state notification status still matters

The Code on Wages, along with the other three Labour Codes, came into force nationally on **21 November 2025**. Central rules were pre-published in draft form on 30-31 December 2025, with final Central rules expected around **April 2026**. However, because labour is a **concurrent subject**, each state must separately notify its own rules to bring the Code's provisions into full operational effect within that state.

As of mid-2026, most states have not yet fully notified their rules. The practical effect is that the Code is legally in force nationally, but employers should continue to check and comply with their **specific state's currently applicable minimum wage notification**, rather than assuming the new central framework has fully superseded existing state rates.

## Staying compliant across multiple states

For employers operating in more than one state, a practical approach is to:

- Maintain a **live register** of applicable minimum wage rates per state, zone, and skill category.
- Set a recurring review (at least twice a year) to check for revisions.
- Track each state's **Labour Code notification status** separately, since implementation timing differs.
- Ensure payroll systems apply the correct rate per employee location, not a single company-wide figure.
