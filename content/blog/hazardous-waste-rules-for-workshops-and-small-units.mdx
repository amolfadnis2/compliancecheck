Any workshop or small unit that generates **used oil, solvents, paint sludge, battery waste or similar residues** is generally covered by India's hazardous waste rules and needs authorisation from the State Pollution Control Board, no matter how small the operation.

## Key facts at a glance

- Hazardous waste in India is governed by the **Hazardous and Other Wastes (Management and Transboundary Movement) Rules**.
- **Authorisation from the State Pollution Control Board (SPCB)** is generally needed for generation, storage, transport, treatment or disposal.
- Common small-unit hazardous waste includes **used lubricating oil, paint sludge, solvents, battery waste, and metal finishing residues**.
- Waste must be sold or transferred only to **SPCB-authorised recyclers, re-refiners, or Common Treatment, Storage and Disposal Facilities (TSDFs)**.
- Authorised units typically must maintain a **waste register** and file an **annual return** with the SPCB.
- Improper disposal can trigger **penalties and remediation liability** that falls on the generator, not just the disposer.

## Why small workshops are often covered

Many small business owners assume hazardous waste rules only apply to large chemical plants, but the rules are triggered by the **nature of the waste**, not the size of the business. A two-person auto repair garage generating used engine oil every week is, in principle, just as covered as a large manufacturing plant - the compliance burden scales down, but the obligation to get authorised does not disappear.

## Common hazardous waste from small units

Workshops and small manufacturing units generate a range of materials that fall under scheduled hazardous waste categories.

| Business type | Typical hazardous waste generated |
| --- | --- |
| Auto workshop / garage | Used lubricating oil, batteries, brake fluid, oily rags |
| Printing unit | Solvents, ink sludge, used cartridges |
| Metal fabrication / workshop | Metal finishing residues, spent acids, paint sludge |
| Electronics repair | E-waste, circuit boards, batteries |
| Small chemical or coating unit | Process residues, contaminated containers |

## Getting authorised

The authorisation process typically involves applying to the SPCB with details of the waste category, expected quantity, and storage/handling arrangements. Authorisation is usually tied to your existing Consent to Operate where applicable, and is renewed on a similar cycle. Very small generators in some states may face a simplified process, but there is no blanket exemption purely based on size - it depends on waste category and state rules.

## Storage and handling requirements

Hazardous waste must generally be stored in a **designated, secured area** with proper labelling, spill containment, and separation from general waste. Storage periods are usually capped - most states expect waste to be sent for authorised disposal within a defined period rather than accumulated indefinitely on-site.

## Where the waste can legally go

This is the step small units most often get wrong. Hazardous waste - especially high-value materials like used oil - cannot simply be sold to any scrap dealer or informal buyer. It must go to an entity that itself holds SPCB authorisation, such as a registered recycler, re-refiner, or a **Common Hazardous Waste Treatment, Storage and Disposal Facility (TSDF)** that pools waste from multiple small generators. Using an unauthorised buyer does not transfer liability away from the generator - it stays with you.

## Records and annual returns

Authorised generators are expected to maintain a running **waste generation and disposal register** and typically file an **annual return** to the SPCB summarising quantities generated, stored, sold, and disposed of during the year. Inspectors commonly ask for this register first, so it is worth treating it as a live document rather than something reconstructed at inspection time.

## The cost of getting it wrong

Improper disposal - dumping used oil into drains, burning waste on-site, or selling to unauthorised buyers - can result in penalties, closure directions, and remediation liability if the site is later found contaminated. Because liability generally attaches to the generator, "we didn't know where it ended up" is rarely an effective defence.
