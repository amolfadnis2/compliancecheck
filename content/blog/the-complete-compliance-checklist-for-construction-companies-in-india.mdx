Construction companies in India carry a heavier compliance load than most sectors because they combine a mobile, often informal workforce with strict welfare and safety law. Missing a registration on even one site can trigger cess demands, work stoppages or contractor liability disputes.

## Key facts at a glance

- The **BOCW cess** is **1% of total construction cost**, payable to the state Building and Other Construction Workers welfare board.
- **BOCW registration** is typically required once a project crosses a state-notified threshold, commonly around **Rs 10 lakh in cost or 10 workers**.
- **Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act** licensing generally applies once **20 or more contract workers** are engaged, though the exact number varies by state.
- **EPF** is mandatory at **20+ employees**, with a wage ceiling of **Rs 15,000/month** and **12% employer plus 12% employee** contribution.
- **ESI** is mandatory at **10+ employees**, with a wage ceiling of **Rs 21,000/month** (**Rs 25,000** for employees with disability) and **0.75% employee / 3.25% employer** contribution.
- A **POSH Internal Committee** is mandatory at any workplace, including a site office, with **10 or more employees**.
- All four **Labour Codes** are in force nationally since **21 November 2025**, but state-level rule notification is still incomplete in most states.

## BOCW registration and cess: non-negotiable for every project

Under the Building and Other Construction Workers (Regulation of Employment and Conditions of Service) Act, employers must register with the state BOCW welfare board once a project crosses the state's notified threshold. Registration funds worker welfare schemes and is checked before final building approvals or occupancy certificates in many states. The **1% cess** on construction cost is usually collected upfront or in instalments by the local authority sanctioning the building plan, so budgeting for it from the tendering stage avoids cash-flow surprises.

## Contract labour licensing: contractor and principal employer duties

Construction relies heavily on subcontracted labour, which brings the Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act into play. Contractors engaging workers above the state threshold need a labour licence, and the principal employer (the builder or developer) must also register the establishment. If a contractor fails to pay wages or provident fund dues, the principal employer can be held liable to make good the shortfall, so verifying contractor compliance documents before awarding work is essential risk management, not paperwork for its own sake.

## EPF and ESI across a mobile, multi-site workforce

Because construction workers move between sites and often work for multiple contractors in a year, EPF and ESI compliance requires careful headcount tracking at the employer level, not just per site. Once an establishment crosses **20 employees** for EPF or **10 employees** for ESI, contributions are mandatory for all eligible workers, including daily-wage and contract labour drawing wages within the ceiling. Many disputes arise simply because employers undercount transient site labour when checking these thresholds.

## Site safety under the new OSH Code

Safety obligations that were earlier scattered across the Factories Act, the BOCW Act and various state rules are being consolidated under the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code. This covers welfare facilities (drinking water, toilets, first aid, crèches on larger sites), working hours, and hazardous-process safeguards. Because state rules under the Code are still being finalised in most places, construction companies should keep following their existing BOCW and state factory-safety obligations until their state confirms the new framework is operative.

## POSH applies at the site office too

It is a common misconception that POSH is only relevant to corporate offices. Any workplace with **10 or more employees**, including a construction site office or project management unit, must have a functioning Internal Committee, display POSH policy notices, and conduct annual awareness training. With courts and regulators pushing for stricter ICC registration and disclosure, construction firms with multiple project sites should confirm whether each site needs its own committee or can be covered centrally.

## Registration and licensing checklist

| Requirement | Trigger | Governing law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| BOCW registration and cess | Project cost/workforce above state threshold | BOCW (RECS) Act, 1996 |
| Contract labour licence | 20+ contract workers (varies by state) | Contract Labour (Regulation and Abolition) Act, 1970 |
| EPF | 20+ employees | EPF and Miscellaneous Provisions Act, 1952 |
| ESI | 10+ employees | ESI Act, 1948 |
| POSH Internal Committee | 10+ employees at any location | POSH Act, 2013 |
| Shops & Establishments registration | Office/site staff, state-specific | State S&E Acts |

## Where the Labour Codes transition stands for construction

The Codes on Wages, Industrial Relations, Social Security, and Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions came into force nationally on **21 November 2025**. Central rules were pre-published in draft in late December 2025, with final Central rules expected around April 2026. Because labour is a concurrent subject, each state must also notify its own rules, and most had not fully done so as of mid-2026. Construction companies should treat this as an in-progress transition and confirm their specific state's notification status rather than assuming the Codes are fully operational everywhere.
