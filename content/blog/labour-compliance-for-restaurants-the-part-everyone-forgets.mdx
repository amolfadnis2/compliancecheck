Most restaurants treat compliance as synonymous with the FSSAI license on the wall, but labour law obligations toward kitchen, floor and delivery staff are just as mandatory and far more commonly missed.

## Key facts at a glance

- **EPF registration** becomes mandatory once a restaurant crosses **20 employees**, with a 12% employee and 12% employer contribution on wages up to the **Rs 15,000/month** ceiling.
- **ESI registration** becomes mandatory at **10 or more employees** (1 or more in certain hazardous categories), for anyone earning up to **Rs 21,000/month** (Rs 25,000 for employees with disability).
- A restaurant with **10 or more employees** must set up an **Internal Committee** under the POSH Act.
- **Gratuity** is generally payable after **5 years of continuous service** at establishments with 10 or more employees.
- State **Shops and Establishments Act** registration is typically the first labour-related registration a restaurant needs, separate from FSSAI.
- Working-hours and overtime rules under state Shops and Establishments Acts apply to restaurant staff just as they do to any other commercial establishment.

## Why labour compliance gets overlooked

Restaurant owners naturally focus energy on food safety because that is what customers and inspectors visibly check first. Labour compliance, by contrast, only surfaces during an inspection, an employee dispute, or a claim - by which point gaps are expensive to fix retroactively. Kitchen staff turnover is often high, shifts are irregular, and many restaurants start as small teams that grow past key headcount thresholds without anyone updating registrations.

## The headcount thresholds that trigger obligations

A lot of restaurant compliance is threshold-driven: nothing is required below a certain headcount, and then several obligations kick in at once. This is the part that gets forgotten because owners set up compliance once at launch and do not revisit it as the team grows.

| Employees | Obligation triggered |
| --- | --- |
| 1+ (varies by state) | Shops and Establishments Act registration |
| 10+ | ESI registration; POSH Internal Committee mandatory; gratuity eligibility applies |
| 20+ | EPF registration mandatory |

Delivery riders and contract kitchen staff often push a restaurant past these thresholds without the owner realizing it, because the headcount that matters is total workforce under the establishment's direction, not just full-time payroll staff.

## Working hours and overtime for restaurant staff

Restaurant shifts are notoriously irregular - split shifts, late closing, weekend surges - which makes working-hours compliance easy to lose track of. State Shops and Establishments Acts typically cap daily and weekly working hours and require overtime payment beyond those limits, along with a weekly off day. Attendance and wage registers need to reflect actual hours worked, not a fixed roster that does not match reality.

## POSH: not optional because the team is small and informal

Many restaurant owners assume POSH obligations apply only to corporate offices. They do not. Once headcount crosses 10, the Internal Committee is mandatory regardless of how casual or family-run the workplace feels, and it must include an external member. Given how much restaurant work happens across shifts and locations with a manager rather than an HR team present, having a documented complaints process matters more here, not less.

## Gratuity and full and final settlement

Restaurant staff turnover means gratuity and full-and-final settlement questions come up often, and get handled inconsistently when there is no written policy. Any employee completing 5 years of continuous service is generally entitled to gratuity on exit, calculated on the standard formula. Restaurants that never formalize this risk disputes at the exact moment they can least afford negative publicity or an inspection.

## Records an inspector will actually ask for

Beyond FSSAI paperwork, a labour inspector visiting a restaurant will typically look for a register of employees, wage records showing statutory deductions, attendance and working-hours records, and evidence of PF/ESI registration and remittance where applicable. Missing or inconsistent registers are one of the most common gaps found during inspections of food businesses, because owners assume the food license alone demonstrates compliance.
