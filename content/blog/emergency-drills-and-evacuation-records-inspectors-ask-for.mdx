Fire and factory inspectors in India typically ask for a **documented drill log, a written evacuation plan, and maintenance records for fire safety equipment** - not just proof that a drill happened once, but a consistent history of drills over time.

## Key facts at a glance

- Most state fire safety rules expect at least **one mock evacuation drill every six months**; high-occupancy or hazardous premises are often expected to drill **quarterly**.
- A **written evacuation plan** with marked exits and assembly points is required at most premises under state fire and building bye-law rules.
- Inspectors commonly request the **drill log register**, **fire NOC status**, and **fire extinguisher/hydrant maintenance records** together, not separately.
- There is **no single national retention period** for drill records, but retaining at least **2-3 years** of records is a safer practice.
- **Building owners** are generally responsible for building-level drills; **individual tenants** (including coworking operators) often need their own occupant participation records too.
- Missing drill records can delay **fire NOC renewal**, independent of whether any actual fire safety lapse occurred.

## Why documentation matters as much as the drill itself

Conducting a fire drill is only half the compliance requirement - the other half is proving you did it, consistently, over time. Inspectors are trained to look for patterns: a single drill record from two years ago signals a box-ticking exercise, while a regular six-monthly cadence signals genuine safety culture. This is why the paperwork often matters as much to an inspector as the drill itself.

## The core records inspectors ask for

### 1. Drill log register

A simple but consistent register recording the date, time, participants, duration, and any observations or corrective actions from each drill. This is usually the first document requested.

### 2. Written evacuation plan

A documented plan showing marked exit routes, assembly points, and responsibilities (who leads evacuation, who does headcounts) - typically displayed at the premises and also kept on file.

### 3. Fire safety equipment maintenance records

Records showing fire extinguishers, hydrants, smoke detectors, and alarm systems have been inspected and serviced on schedule, usually by a licensed vendor.

### 4. Fire NOC and renewal status

The **No Objection Certificate** issued by the local fire department, along with proof it has been renewed on time - an expired NOC is one of the most common findings in inspections.

## Recommended drill frequency by premises type

| Premises type | Typical drill frequency expected |
| --- | --- |
| General office / commercial establishment | Every 6 months |
| High-occupancy building (mall, large office) | Quarterly |
| Factory / hazardous unit | Quarterly or more frequent |
| Coworking space (building-level) | Every 6 months, plus tenant-level participation logs |

Exact frequency requirements are set at the state level and can vary by occupancy classification, so always confirm with your local fire department's specific rules.

## Retention: how long to keep records

There is no uniform national rule specifying exactly how many years of drill records must be retained, which is itself a common source of confusion. In practice, most fire departments expect at least the **current and previous year's** records to be readily available, but retaining **2-3 years** of history gives a stronger compliance position and helps demonstrate a consistent safety pattern rather than a reactive one.

## Multi-tenant buildings and coworking spaces

In shared buildings, evacuation drills are usually organised at the **building level** by the owner or facility manager, covering all occupants. However, individual tenants - including coworking operators managing their own membership base - are often still expected to maintain their own records of employee or member participation, safety briefings, and any internal fire warden training, separate from the building's overall drill documentation.

## What happens if records are missing

Beyond the immediate inspection finding, missing or inconsistent drill records can delay **fire NOC renewal**, which in turn can affect other approvals tied to it, such as trade licences or occupancy certificates in some states. In the event of an actual incident, the absence of drill records can also be used as evidence of inadequate safety preparedness.
