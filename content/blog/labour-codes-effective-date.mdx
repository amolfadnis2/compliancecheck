India has been on the cusp of its most sweeping labour law reform in seven decades. Four new Labour Codes consolidate **29 central acts** into a unified framework designed to modernise workplaces, simplify compliance, and extend social security to unorganised workers. Yet as of mid-2026, implementation remains uneven across states — and that patchwork creates real compliance risk for Indian SMEs.

This guide cuts through the confusion: what the four codes say, where states stand today, what changes in practice, and the 90-day checklist every HR team should run through.

---

## The Four Codes at a Glance

Parliament passed all four codes between 2019 and 2020. They replace the following central legislation:

| Code | Year Passed | Acts Consolidated |
|------|------------|-------------------|
| **Code on Wages, 2019** | 2019 | Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Wages Act, Payment of Bonus Act, Equal Remuneration Act |
| **Industrial Relations Code, 2020** | 2020 | Trade Unions Act, Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, Industrial Disputes Act |
| **Social Security Code, 2020** | 2020 | EPF Act, ESI Act, Maternity Benefit Act, Gratuity Act, EDLI Act + 4 others |
| **OSH, Working Conditions & Service Code, 2020** | 2020 | Factories Act, Mines Act, BOCW Act + 10 others |

<Callout type="info">
**Key principle:** The new codes rely on **delegated legislation** — the substantive provisions are in the codes, but the operational rules (threshold employee counts, benefit rates, contribution formulae) come via state-specific rules. States must frame and notify these rules before the codes apply in their territory.
</Callout>

## Current Implementation Status

### What the Central Government Has Done

The Ministry of Labour & Employment published the **draft central rules** for all four codes by 2021–22. These central rules serve as a template for states but bind only central-sphere establishments (railways, mines, ONGC, etc.).

### State-Level Picture

As of May 2026, **fewer than 15 states** have fully notified rules for all four codes. Most major commercial states — Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana — have notified rules for **some but not all** codes.

<Callout type="warning">
**Why this matters:** A code does not apply to a private establishment in your state until that state has both enacted the code in its legislative calendar AND notified the associated rules. Check your state's Labour Department gazette for the latest status.
</Callout>

**States with broad notification (all 4 codes):** Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand (as of Q1 2026 — verify before acting).

**States with partial notification:** Maharashtra (Wages + IR Codes), Karnataka (Wages Code), Tamil Nadu (pending), Delhi (pending).

**Pending full notification:** West Bengal, Kerala, Odisha, Jharkhand (all four codes pending as of May 2026).

## What Changes for SMEs

### 1. Wages Code — Biggest Immediate Impact

The Wages Code redefines **"wages"** narrowly: only Basic + DA + retaining allowance count as "wages" for purposes of minimum wage compliance, bonus, and overtime. All other allowances are excluded — capped collectively at 50% of total CTC.

**Practical impact:** If your current CTC structure has allowances exceeding 50% of total pay, you will need to rebalance. This affects take-home pay calculations, bonus liability, and gratuity.

- **Minimum Wage:** A floor wage concept applies — no state can set a minimum wage below the central floor.
- **Payment timelines:** Wages must be paid by the **7th** (for monthly-paid workers in establishments with 1,000+ employees) or the **10th** (all others). Existing 7th-of-month payroll cycles are safe; anything later is not.

### 2. Social Security Code — ESIC & EPF Threshold Changes

The Social Security Code is expected to **lower the EPF threshold** from 20 employees to potentially 10 (subject to state rules). It also brings **gig and platform workers** under a new social security framework.

- Maternity benefit: Extended to all establishments (currently Maternity Benefit Act applies to 10+ employees; the Code may remove the threshold).
- Gratuity: The minimum service threshold for gratuity may be **reduced from 5 years to 1 year** for fixed-term contract employees — a significant cost implication for establishments using contract workers.

### 3. Industrial Relations Code — Standing Orders

Under the existing Industrial Employment (Standing Orders) Act, only establishments with **100+ workmen** must maintain certified standing orders. The IR Code raises this threshold to **300 workmen**. Establishments with 100–299 workers will no longer be legally required to get standing orders certified — but model standing orders will apply by default.

**Practical implication:** If you have between 100–299 employees and currently have certified standing orders, you do NOT need to abandon them. But if you are below 300 and setting up for the first time, you may skip the certification process under the new code (where notified).

### 4. OSH Code — Unified Inspection

The OSH Code creates a **unified inspection scheme** across factories, shops, construction sites, and contract workers. Inspectors will be appointed under a single framework rather than across multiple acts.

For manufacturing SMEs, the code extends many Factories Act provisions to **establishments with 10+ workers** (power-using) or **20+ workers** (non-power), broadly similar to the existing act.

- [ ] Visit your state Labour Department website and search the official gazette for Labour Code notifications

- [ ] Note the commencement date — most states specify an effective date in the notification

- [ ] Map every pay component: Basic, DA, HRA, LTA, Medical, Special Allowance, etc.

- [ ] Confirm total headcount including contract workers and fixed-term employees

- [ ] Review fixed-term contracts: assess gratuity liability if 1-year threshold applies

- [ ] Review your existing standing orders (if any) — will they be valid under the new code?
- [ ] Update employment contract templates to reflect new definitions of "wages"
- [ ] Update the HR handbook to reference the new code provisions

**Week 9–12: Training and Systems**
- [ ] Brief your payroll vendor or in-house payroll team on wage definition changes
- [ ] Update payroll software configuration if needed
- [ ] Train HR managers on new IR Code procedures (grievance handling, dispute resolution)
- [ ] Document your compliance posture for audit readiness

## FAQ

**Q: Do the Labour Codes replace state labour laws too?**

No. The four Labour Codes consolidate **central** legislation. State labour laws (state-specific shops and establishments acts, state minimum wage schedules, etc.) remain in force. However, states must bring their legislation in alignment with the central codes over time.

**Q: My company has fewer than 10 employees. Am I affected?**

The Wages Code applies to **all establishments** — there is no size exemption for minimum wage compliance or payment timelines. The Social Security Code thresholds vary by benefit type and state rules. The OSH and IR Codes have higher thresholds that may not apply to very small establishments.

**Q: What happens if a state has not notified rules yet?**

You continue to follow the existing (pre-Code) central and state acts until the new rules are notified and come into force. Do not pre-emptively restructure wages or contracts before your state notifies.

**Q: Can I get a definitive answer on my state's status?**

The Ministry of Labour publishes an updated state-wise tracker on [labour.gov.in](https://labour.gov.in). Additionally, your state Labour Commissioner's office maintains the official gazette. Always rely on primary sources rather than secondary summaries (including this article).
